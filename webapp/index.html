    Guide How to train a character well

    Image count

    30–80 photos is the practical sweet spot. Variety beats quantity — 50 well-chosen images across shot types beats 100 portraits-only. Below 25 the LoRA overfits to whatever pose dominates; above 100–150 you start hitting diminishing returns and risk contradictory signals (e.g. half-bearded vs half-not). Training time stays roughly the same regardless of dataset size — it's fixed by step count, not image count.

    Shot type coverage — biggest single lever

    The LoRA can only generate what it saw. If your set is all torso-up portraits, long shots will look mushy because the model never learned what this person looks like at distance. Aim for this mix on a 50–80 image set:

    • 5–8 tight close-ups · face fills frame, anchors identity
    • 15–25 medium close-ups · head + shoulders, the workhorse
    • 8–12 medium shots · waist up, teaches body proportions
    • 10–15 wide / full-body shots · the missing piece in most datasets — without these, long shots in T2V come out distorted or texture-mushy
    • 2–3 extreme long shots · figure at distance, environment dominates

    Image aspect ratio

    Use whatever native aspect ratio the photos came in — portrait, landscape, square, all fine. The trainer's letterbox crop preserves the original proportions (it adds black bars to fit the training canvas rather than chopping the sides off). The model learns the bars are a constant background factor across every sample and ignores them at inference.

    Lighting variety

    Mix indoor and outdoor, warm and cool, day and night, key-light from different angles. If every photo shares studio lighting, the LoRA bakes that lighting into every generation.

    Background variety

    Different rooms, streets, contexts. A single backdrop teaches the LoRA to refuse to put the character anywhere else.

    Avoid

    • group photos (the LoRA can't tell which face is the subject)
    • obvious props that aren't part of the look
    • heavy makeup that varies wildly between shots
    • face partly obscured (hand over mouth, microphone in front of face)
    • blurry / out-of-focus shots — training amplifies blur

    Caption rule

    Describe what varies in each shot — outfit, setting, pose, lighting, framing. Don't describe invariant identity features (face shape, hair color, build); the LoRA absorbs those automatically. The auto-caption button uses Gemma 3 to generate [VISUAL]:-format captions following this rule.

    Frame count

    20–100+ hand-picked movie frames. More variety inside the look (different scenes, different subjects) helps generalization. Skip duplicate-ish shots from the same scene — they teach less than fewer-but-more-varied frames.

    What makes a frame "in the style"

    • Composition · framing, headroom, negative space, geometry
    • Color grading · palette, contrast, white point, saturation
    • Lighting · key direction, hardness, motivation, falloff
    • Lens character · focal length, depth of field, optical traits
    • Mood · the atmospheric thumbprint the user is after

    Consistency beats variety

    Opposite of a character dataset. You want every frame to share the visual fingerprint you're training on. Mixed grades and lighting motivations dilute the style — the LoRA averages everything you show it.

    Avoid

    • frames where the subject's identity dominates the frame
    • watermarks, subtitles, channel logos
    • heavily compressed or low-bitrate source frames
    • extreme aspect crops (letterboxed black bars hurt)

    Caption rule

    Describe the shot itself, not who's in it — composition, color grading, lighting, mood. The trigger anchors the look; the caption body teaches the model what to associate with that trigger.

If the mouth doesn't move: (1) start on a closed-mouth, face-forward picture — an open-mouth anchor tends to freeze open (the panel warns when it detects one); (2) remove "static" / "holds still" / "motionless" language from the prompt — a stillness word freezes the whole scene, mouth included; (3) for a song, try the vocal-only option so the model listens to the voice instead of the full mix.
